Conduit Saddles and Kicks: 3-Point Saddle Basics and Shrink

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

When conduit has to cross a pipe or duct, you use a saddle. When it has to turn a small angle at a wall or box, you use a kick. Both reuse the offset multipliers you already know.

A kick

A kick is a single bend of a small angle, used to line conduit up with a box knockout or to change direction slightly. Because there is only one bend, there is no pair of marks: you set the distance from the bend to the box and choose the angle. The shrink constants are the same as for an offset, so a kick of height H at 30° loses about H × 1/4 in. of length, and you allow for that on the leg past the kick.

The 3-point saddle

A 3-point saddle is two half-offsets back to back. The center bend is 45° and the two outer bends are 22.5° each, in the opposite direction. Geometrically the outer legs climb at 22.5°, so the exact spacing from the center mark to each outer mark is the obstruction height × 2.613. Many field references round this to 2.5, and others to 2.6.

ItemRule
Center bend45°
Outer bends22.5° each, opposite direction
Outer marks2.5 × obstruction height on each side of the center mark
Shrink3/16 in. per inch of height
Center mark positionCenter of obstruction plus the shrink

Worked example: crossing a 2 in. pipe

Obstruction height (outside the pipe plus conduit clearance): 2 in.

  • Outer marks: 2.5 × 2 = 5 in. each side of the center mark, so 10 in. overall.
  • Shrink: 2 × 3/16 = 3/8 in. total.
  • Bend the center mark to 45°, then the two outer marks to 22.5° in the opposite direction, keeping the conduit flat on the floor between bends.

Saddle or offset?

Use an offset when conduit changes level and stays on the new level, for example entering a box. Use a saddle when it must go over something and come back to the original level. A 4-point saddle works better for large round obstructions, and you can read more bend types on the offset multiplier chart.

Tips for accurate saddles

  1. Mark the center of the obstruction on the conduit first, then the outer marks.
  2. Line up the center-of-bend notch on the center mark and the arrow on the outer marks, following your bender’s instructions.
  3. Check that the conduit stays in one plane; twisting between bends is the usual cause of a bad saddle.
  4. Test fit with a piece of scrap before bending expensive conduit.

Mind the 360° limit between pull points. A saddle uses 45° + 22.5° + 22.5° = 90° of that allowance. Confirm sizing with the conduit fill calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How is a 3-point saddle bent?
With a 45° center bend and two 22.5° outer bends in the opposite direction.
How far apart are the saddle marks?
The outer marks sit 2.5 times the obstruction height on each side of the center mark (exact 2.613).
What is the shrink for a saddle?
About 3/16 inch per inch of obstruction height.
What is a kick in conduit bending?
A single small-angle bend used to line up conduit with a box or change direction slightly.
When do I use a saddle instead of an offset?
When the conduit must cross an obstruction and return to its original level.
