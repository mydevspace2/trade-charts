Conduit Offset Angles: 30° vs 45° vs 22.5° (Multipliers and Shrink)

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

An offset moves conduit over an obstruction with two equal bends. The angle you pick changes how far apart the bends sit, how much length the offset eats, and how easy it is to pull wire. Here is how to choose between the common angles.

The geometry in one line

An offset is a right triangle. The offset depth is the side opposite the bend angle and the distance between your two marks is the long diagonal. So distance between bends = depth × 1 ÷ sin(angle). That factor is the multiplier. Because sin 30° is exactly 0.5, the 30° multiplier is exactly 2.

Field multipliers are the rounded numbers printed on benders. They are close enough for small offsets, but the rounding error grows with depth. At 10° the field value of 6 is about 4% longer than the exact 5.759, which is nearly an inch on a 12 in. offset.

Worked example: a 6 in. offset

AngleMarks apartShrink (field)
22.5°6 × 2.6 = 15.6 in.6 × 3/16 = 1 1/8 in.

45°6 × 1.414 = 8 1/2 in.6 × 3/8 = 2 1/4 in.

Shrink is the length the run loses because the conduit takes the diagonal instead of going straight. You add the shrink to your overall measurement so the conduit still reaches the next box.

Which angle should you choose?

  • 30°: the default. The math is a doubling, shrink is a clean quarter inch per inch, and the offset is gentle enough for wire pulls.
  • 45°: use when space is tight and the marks must be close together. Shrink is larger, and a steeper offset pulls harder, so avoid it on long runs with many conductors.
  • 22.5°: use for shallow, long transitions, and as the side bends of a saddle. It needs the most room between marks.
  • 10°: only for very slight jogs. Small angle errors turn into big position errors, so it is hard to be accurate.

How to bend the offset

  1. Measure the offset depth from the conduit centerline to where it needs to be.
  2. Pick the angle and multiply the depth by its multiplier to get the mark spacing.
  3. Mark the conduit at the first bend position and the second mark the calculated distance away.
  4. Bend the first mark to the chosen angle, then rotate the conduit 180° so the bends stay in one plane, and bend the second mark to the same angle.
  5. Lay the conduit on a flat surface to check that the legs are parallel and nothing twisted.

Dedicated pages: 30°, 45°, 22.5°, or the full offset multiplier chart.

Bends and code limits

Whatever angle you use, the NEC limits bends between pull points to 360° total for EMT and rigid conduit, and requires bends that do not reduce the conduit’s internal diameter. Each offset uses twice its angle toward the 360° total, so a 30° offset counts 60°. Check the adopted code in your jurisdiction, and use the EMT fill chart to confirm the conduit is sized for the wire.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting shrink and ending up short at the next box.
  • Letting the conduit roll between bends so the offset twists.
  • Mixing field and exact multipliers within one run.
  • Marking to the edge of the obstruction instead of the centerline of the conduit.

Frequently asked questions

What is the multiplier for a 30 degree offset?
Exactly 2. Multiply the offset depth by 2 to get the distance between the bend marks.
What is the shrink for a 45 degree offset?
About 3/8 inch per inch of offset depth (exact value 0.414 in.).
Which offset angle is easiest to pull wire through?
Shallower angles such as 22.5° and 30° are easier on conductors than 45°.
Why is the 10 degree multiplier 6?
The exact value is 5.759; benders print 6 as an easy rounded number, which adds about 4% error.
How many degrees does an offset use toward the 360 degree limit?
Twice its bend angle, so a 30° offset counts 60° toward the limit between pull points.
