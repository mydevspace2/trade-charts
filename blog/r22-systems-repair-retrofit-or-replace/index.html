R-22 Systems Today: Repair, Retrofit or Replace?

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

R-22 has not been made in the United States since the start of 2020, yet millions of R-22 air conditioners are still running. The rules are less dramatic than the headlines: you may keep using an R-22 system, you may keep servicing it, and there is no drop-in refrigerant if you decide to change. Here is what EPA actually says and how to think about the choice.

What the rules say

  • U.S. production and import of R-22 (HCFC-22) ended on January 1, 2020. You may continue to use R-22 in an existing system for as long as necessary.
  • Servicing continues from recovered and reclaimed R-22 and from stock produced before 2020. EPA expects it to be available well beyond 2020, but notes that price and availability may change.
  • Equipment holding R-22 could not be manufactured after January 1, 2010. A central air conditioner on the original R-22 charge is therefore at least 16 years old in 2026.
  • EPA does not require homeowners to replace or convert an R-22 system.
  • Only Section 608-certified technicians can buy and handle the refrigerant. It is illegal to knowingly vent it.
  • If an outdoor unit fails, EPA says owners may replace the condensing unit, as long as the R-22 unit meets the regional efficiency standard.

There is no drop-in replacement

EPA is blunt about this: there are no “drop-in” replacements for HCFC-22, and alternative refrigerants will not work well without changes to system components. A retrofit is allowed only with a refrigerant EPA has listed as acceptable under the SNAP program for that use. The technician must recover the remaining R-22 first, follow the manufacturer’s guidelines for the new refrigerant, and explain how performance and energy use may change. Most leak repairs end up recharged with R-22, because that avoids the changes a conversion needs.

Never use “R-22a” or similar products sold as a cheap R-22 substitute. EPA says R-22a is a flammable hydrocarbon blend, not an acceptable alternative, and an R-22 system has no safety features for flammability.

R-410A runs far higher than R-22: about 130.1 psig on the suction side at 45°F versus 76.1 psig for R-22, and 341.9 versus 210.8 psig on the liquid side at 105°F. An R-22 system is not built for those pressures, so R-410A is not a retrofit for an R-22 system; it needs a new outdoor unit and a matched indoor coil, and new R-410A equipment is itself being replaced by lower-GWP refrigerants. Retrofit blends such as R-407C, R-422D and R-438A run at pressures close to R-22, but they have temperature glide, so you must charge by liquid and compare against the dew or bubble column that matches the side you are reading. Repair, retrofit or replace?

This is a judgement call, and EPA leaves it to the owner. Questions that settle most cases:

  1. Where is the leak, and can it be fixed? EPA recommends finding and repairing leaks instead of topping off. If the leak is in the evaporator or condenser coil, the repair may cost more than the equipment is worth.
  2. How old is the system? A central system built to run on R-22 was built before 2010, so it is at least 16 years old. The DOE puts central air conditioner life at 15 to 20 years, so it is near the end either way. Check the serial number using how old is my AC unit.
  3. What is a pound of R-22 costing now? Ask for a written price for the charge and for the repair, since the price changes.
  4. What would a retrofit change? Ask the contractor which SNAP-listed refrigerant, what component changes, and what happens to capacity and efficiency.
  5. What does the replacement cost? A new system matched to a current refrigerant is the long-term fix, and EPA says an ENERGY STAR model can cut annual energy bills by more than $160, depending on where you live.

A small leak on a sound system that is otherwise reliable can justify a repair and recharge for a few more years. A coil leak, a compressor failure or repeated top-ups are the situations where owners most often weigh replacement. A retrofit can make sense where replacing everything is costly, as long as the contractor can name the SNAP-listed refrigerant and the component changes.

If you are the technician

Recover the existing refrigerant before opening the circuit, record the amount, and use a leak check before recharging. Do not top off an R-22 system with a different refrigerant; mixing contaminates the charge. When retrofitting, label the system with the new refrigerant and use the correct PT column for that blend.

Frequently asked questions

Is R-22 illegal to use?
No. EPA says you may continue to use R-22 in an existing system for as long as necessary. Only new production and import ended on January 1, 2020.
Can I still get R-22 for service?
Yes, from recovered, reclaimed and pre-2020 stock, handled by Section 608 certified technicians. EPA notes price and availability may change.
Is there a drop-in replacement for R-22?
No. EPA says there are no drop-in replacements and that alternatives will not work well without changes to components.
Can I put R-410A in an R-22 system?
No. R-410A runs at much higher pressure and needs equipment designed for it, so it requires a new outdoor unit and matched coil.
What is R-22a and is it safe?
R-22a is a flammable hydrocarbon blend marketed as an R-22 substitute. EPA says it is not an acceptable alternative.
