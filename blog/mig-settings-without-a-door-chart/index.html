MIG Settings Without a Door Chart: Amps, Wire Speed and Voltage

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

Most MIG welders have a chart inside the wire door. Secondhand machines, older units and some shop rigs do not, and then you need a starting point you can trust. Miller Electric publishes a simple three-step method for solid-wire steel. It takes about a minute and gets you close enough to fine-tune on scrap.

Step 1: amps from material thickness

Miller’s rule of thumb is that each 0.001 inch of material thickness needs about 1 amp of output. Material 0.125 inch (1/8 in) thick needs about 125 amps; 0.250 inch needs about 250 amps. Convert gauge to inches first: 22 ga steel is about 0.030 in, 16 ga about 0.060 in, 14 ga about 0.075 in and 12 ga about 0.105 in. Our MIG settings calculator does the conversion for you.

Step 2: pick the wire size from the amperage

Miller ties wire diameter to the amperage range it handles well. To avoid constant wire changes, choose the size that suits the thickness you weld most often.

.023 in30–130 A
.030 in40–145 A
.035 in50–180 A
.045 in75–250 A

Step 3: wire feed speed from the multiplier

Wire feed speed controls amperage on most machines. Without a chart, multiply the amps by a factor for your wire size to get inches per minute (ipm).

Where two wire sizes overlap, either works; the table shows the first match, so check the multiplier table if you prefer a different wire. For example, 125 A on 1/8 in plate fits .030 in wire at 250 ipm, .035 in wire at 200 ipm, or .045 in at 125 ipm.

Step 4: set the voltage by sound and look

Miller says voltage changes the height and width of the bead. Set the wire speed first, then adjust voltage for a crisp, stable arc. If the arc stubs into the work, raise the voltage. If it is erratic and burns back at the tip, lower it. Too much voltage gives poor arc control, inconsistent penetration and a turbulent puddle. Too little gives poor starts, spatter, a convex bead and poor tie-in at the toes. For a starting voltage by wire size and amperage, see the voltage chart on the calculator page.

Read the bead to fine-tune

  • Narrow, convex bead, poor tie-in: amperage too low, or travel too fast.
  • Wide bead, burn-through on thin metal: amperage too high, or travel too slow.
  • Porosity and pinholes: little or no shielding gas.

Change one setting at a time and note the values that work. Write them on tape inside the door and you have your own chart.

Limits of this method

The rule applies to solid wire on steel. Aluminum, stainless and flux-core need different settings, as do out-of-position welds and very thick joints. Your machine’s manual and chart come first, and any weld that carries load or must meet a code needs a qualified procedure, not a rule of thumb. Frequently asked questions

How many amps do I need to MIG weld 1/8 inch steel?
About 125 A by Miller’s rule of 1 amp per 0.001 inch of thickness.
What wire speed for .030 wire?
Multiply the amps by 2 to get inches per minute. At 125 A that is 250 ipm.
Which MIG wire size should I use?
Miller lists .023 in for 30–130 A, .030 in for 40–145 A, .035 in for 50–180 A and .045 in for 75–250 A.
How do I know if my voltage is right?
Aim for a crisp, stable arc. If it stubs into the work, raise voltage; if it is erratic and burns at the tip, lower it.
Does this work for aluminum or flux-core?
No. The rules are for solid wire on steel. Use the machine manual for other wires and metals.
