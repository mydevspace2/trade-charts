R-454B Service Basics for R-410A Techs: Pressures, Tools, Charging

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

R-454B is the refrigerant most US residential split-system manufacturers moved to when new equipment had to drop below 700 GWP. For a tech who knows R-410A the good news is that it runs at similar pressures with the same POE oil. The differences are small but they matter: slightly lower pressures for the same coil temperature, a little glide, and the A2L (mildly flammable) safety rules.

Why R-454B is showing up on your calls

Under the EPA Technology Transitions rule, new residential and light commercial air conditioners and heat pumps manufactured or imported from January 1, 2025 must use a refrigerant with a GWP below 700. R-410A (GWP 2,088) does not qualify; R-454B does. In May 2026 EPA finalized a revision that removed the January 1, 2026 installation deadline for R-410A systems built or imported before 2025, so you will keep seeing both refrigerants side by side for years: new R-454B installs, existing R-410A systems, and leftover R-410A inventory still being installed. Repairs of existing R-410A systems are not restricted by these rules.

Pressures: close to R-410A, but not the same

This is the part that trips people up. At the same saturation temperature R-454B runs a few psi lower than R-410A. At a 40 °F evaporator R-410A sits at about 118.4 psig, while R-454B shows 107.5 psig at the dew point. At a 100 °F condensing temperature R-410A is about 318.5 psig and R-454B about 303.3 psig at the bubble point.

Turn it around and the risk is clear. If an R-454B system shows 118 psig on the suction and you read it on an R-410A scale, you will call it 40 °F. On the R-454B dew column 118 psig is about 45 °F, so the superheat you calculate is several degrees too high and you may add refrigerant to a system that does not need it. On the high side, 300 psig is about 99 °F bubble for R-454B versus 96 °F for R-410A, which shifts subcooling by roughly the same amount. Always set the digital manifold to R-454B or use the R-454B PT chart.

Glide: small, but use the right column

R-454B is a zeotropic blend. At a 40 °F evaporator the dew and bubble pressures differ by about 5.7 psi, a glide of roughly 2.7 °F on our chart data. That is small compared with R-407C, but large enough that you should use the dew column for superheat and the bubble column for subcooling. Our superheat and subcooling calculators pick the right column automatically. For background see temperature glide explained.

Tools: the core four

JB Industries and Carrier’s A2L tools catalog both list the same four tools that touch the refrigerant circuit and must be A2L-compatible: manifolds and gauges (with R-454B scales or updated refrigerant tables), vacuum pumps (non-sparking), recovery machines (non-sparking) and electronic leak detectors (sealed so they cannot spark). Tools that never touch the refrigerant usually carry over. Details are in our guide to A2L tools.

Cylinders and hoses

  • A2L recovery and refrigerant cylinders have a red band or red top, a pressure relief valve instead of a rupture disc, and left-hand (reverse) threads.
  • You need a reverse-thread adapter to connect standard hoses to them.
  • Recover R-454B only into a cylinder marked for it; never mix it with R-410A, even though the pressures look similar.
  • Separate hoses (or careful purging) avoid cross-contamination between R-410A and R-454B systems.

Charging an R-454B system step by step

  1. Read the outdoor unit rating plate and installation manual. They give the factory charge, the line-set adjustment and the target subcooling.
  2. Remove ignition sources from the work area and ventilate it.
  3. Evacuate with an A2L-rated pump and confirm the vacuum with a micron gauge.
  4. Weigh in the charge as a liquid (it is a blend, so liquid charging keeps the composition right).
  5. Run the system at least 15 minutes, then check subcooling against the rating plate (most R-454B splits use a TXV or EEV) and confirm superheat is reasonable.
  6. Adjust in small amounts, letting the system settle between changes.

Refrigerant detection systems

Many R-454B indoor units include a refrigerant detection system (RDS): a sensor that, above a set concentration, turns on the indoor blower to dilute a leak and locks out the compressor or heat. UL 60335-2-40 requires these on systems whose charge exceeds a limit tied to the refrigerant’s flammability. Wire and test the sensor exactly as the installation instructions say; a disconnected sensor can leave the system in a lockout or without its leak protection.

What does not change

Airflow first, clean coils, correct line-set sizing, nitrogen while brazing on an open and recovered system, triple-checking the rating plate: good R-410A habits carry straight over. Compare the two refrigerants side by side on our R-454B vs R-410A page.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use my R-410A gauges on R-454B?
The pressures are in the same range, but the saturation scales differ. Use a digital manifold set to R-454B or analog gauges with an R-454B scale, and keep separate hoses or purge them to avoid cross-contamination.
Is R-454B a drop-in for R-410A?
No. R-454B is used in equipment designed and listed for it as an A2L refrigerant. Do not add it to an R-410A system or mix the two.
Does R-454B use the same oil as R-410A?
Yes, both use POE oil.
Is R-454B charged as a liquid?
Yes. It is a zeotropic blend, so it is charged as liquid from the cylinder to keep its composition correct.
Can R-410A systems still be installed?
EPA’s May 2026 final rule removed the January 1, 2026 installation deadline for residential and light commercial systems built or imported before January 1, 2025, so that inventory can be installed until it runs out. New equipment manufactured from 2025 must be below 700 GWP.
