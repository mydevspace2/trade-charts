Refrigerant Temperature Glide: Why Blends Have Two Pressures

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

Open a PT chart for R-407C or R-448A and you will see two pressure columns for every temperature. That is temperature glide: a blend does not boil at one temperature. Understanding it is the difference between a correct charge and one that is several degrees off.

What glide is

A pure refrigerant such as R-32 or R-134a boils and condenses at a single temperature for a given pressure. A zeotropic blend (the 400-series refrigerants) is a mix of refrigerants with different boiling points. At constant pressure the more volatile component boils off first, the remaining liquid gets richer in the less volatile one, and its boiling temperature rises. The blend starts to boil at the bubble point and finishes at the dew point. The difference between them is the temperature glide. Azeotropic blends (500-series, such as R-507A and R-513A) behave like a single refrigerant and have essentially no glide.

Notice the split: R-410A, R-404A, R-507A and R-513A act like single refrigerants, while most R-22 and R-404A replacements (R-407C, R-427A, R-438A, R-448A, R-449A, R-407A/F) glide 7–11 °F, and the low-GWP refrigeration blends R-454A and R-455A glide even more.

Which column to use

  • Superheat starts where the last liquid boils off, so use the dew temperature at suction pressure.
  • Subcooling starts where the vapor has fully condensed, so use the bubble temperature at liquid pressure.

If you use a digital manifold, check that it is set to the exact blend on the rating plate. R-407A, R-407C and R-407F share the same three components in different proportions, and their charts show different pressures at the same temperature, so a near miss on the refrigerant setting gives the wrong saturation temperatures.

Sporlan, Chemours and National Refrigerants all give the same rule. Our superheat and subcooling calculators apply it automatically.

Worked example: R-407C

Suction pressure 63.2 psig. On the R-407C PT chart that is 40 °F dew, but only about 29 °F bubble. With a 52 °F suction line, superheat is 12 °F using the dew column. Using the bubble column by mistake gives 23 °F, which looks like a starving evaporator and could lead you to overcharge.

On the liquid side at 260.2 psig, the bubble temperature is 110 °F and the dew temperature about 119 °F. With a 100 °F liquid line, subcooling is 10 °F on the bubble column; the dew column would claim about 19 °F.

Glide in the coil

In a real evaporator the refrigerant enters as a mix of liquid and vapor, so the coil inlet is already part way along the glide; the coil temperature rises from inlet to outlet even with no pressure drop. Some compressor makers rate blends at a mid-point (average) temperature rather than the dew point, which is why catalog evaporating temperatures for high-glide blends can look a few degrees different from your gauge. For field charging, stick to dew for superheat and bubble for subcooling.

Fractionation and charging

Because the vapor and liquid of a zeotropic blend have different compositions, taking vapor out of a cylinder changes what is left in it. Charge blends as liquid from the cylinder (meter it into the suction side slowly so it flashes before the compressor).

Retrofits

When an R-22 system is retrofitted to a high-glide blend such as R-407C or R-438A, the TXV may need adjustment, and the pressures you are used to no longer mean the same thing. Compare the two directly on R-407C vs R-22 and R-438A vs R-422D. For the basics of reading two columns, see how to read a PT chart.

Frequently asked questions

What is temperature glide?
The difference between the bubble point and the dew point of a zeotropic blend at the same pressure. It happens because the blend’s components boil at different temperatures.
Which refrigerants have glide?
Zeotropic 400-series blends. R-407C, R-448A, R-449A and R-427A glide around 10 °F; R-410A and R-404A glide less than 1 °F. Pure refrigerants and 500-series azeotropes have essentially none.
Do I use dew or bubble for superheat?
Dew for superheat, bubble for subcooling.
Why must blends be charged as liquid?
The vapor in the cylinder has a different composition from the liquid. Charging vapor changes the composition in the system and in the cylinder; this is called fractionation.
What is a near-azeotrope?
A blend with a small glide, under about 5 °F, where glide and fractionation have little practical effect, such as R-410A and R-404A.
