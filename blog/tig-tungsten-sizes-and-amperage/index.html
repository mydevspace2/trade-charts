TIG Tungsten Sizes and Amperage: What Fits Which Current

Guide · Updated November 21, 2026

Choosing a TIG tungsten comes down to one question: how many amps will you run? Too thin an electrode for the current overheats and balls up or melts; too thick makes the arc hard to start and unstable at low amps. This guide gives the current range for each common diameter, a starting amperage by steel thickness and a worked example.

Current range by tungsten diameter

The ranges below follow Miller Electric’s published amperage table for tungsten electrodes. The first column covers 2% alloyed tungsten (thoriated, ceriated or lanthanated) on DC electrode negative; the second covers pure tungsten.

Tungsten diameter2% alloyed tungstenPure tungsten
1/16 in (1.6 mm)50–140 A60–90 A
3/32 in (2.4 mm)125–200 A125–160 A
1/8 in (3.2 mm)150–325 A190–240 A
3/16 in (4.8 mm)300–340 A260–320 A
1/4 in (6.4 mm)—330–450 A

Ranges overlap because the best choice depends on duty cycle, joint and preference. Many welders pick the smaller electrode near the low end of a range for easier starts, and step up when running near the top.

Starting amps by thickness

A common starting point for steel is about 1 amp per 0.001 in of thickness, the same guideline Miller publishes for MIG. With a foot pedal you can then ease up or down.

Worked example

You are welding 1/8 in mild steel. The rule gives about 125 A. Both 1/16 in (50–140 A) and 3/32 in (125–200 A) cover that figure, but 125 A sits at the very bottom of the 3/32 in range and near the top of the 1/16 in range. On a long weld that heats the electrode, 3/32 in is the safer choice for the current you expect to ride at. Start at 125 A, then raise it if the puddle is slow to form.

Newer inverter-era ranges are higher

Miller’s later owner’s manuals for inverter machines list higher ceilings for 2% ceriated, lanthanated or thoriated tungsten on DC electrode negative: 1/16 in 70–150 A (some manuals 50–160 A), 3/32 in 150–250 A and 1/8 in 250–400 A. The table above is the more conservative older guide, so it is a safe place to start. Check your machine’s manual for the exact range it supports.

Which tungsten type?

Miller’s 2026 knowledge-hub article says pure tungsten is no longer the recommended choice for AC TIG on inverter machines, because rare-earth alloys such as ceriated and lanthanated tungsten outperform it. Miller’s manuals also suggest considering ceria, lanthana or yttria instead of thoria, since grinding dust from thoriated electrodes contains low-level radioactive material, so use local exhaust or a respirator when grinding.

Setup basics

  • Grind lengthwise to a point for DC so the arc stays stable and focused.
  • Typical argon flow is about 15–25 cfh. More is not better and can pull air into the arc.
  • Use filler rod no thicker than the base metal on thin sheet.
  • Stainless usually needs a little less current than carbon steel; aluminum on AC needs more, with balance and frequency set per your machine’s guide.

Use the full TIG amperage chart for more thicknesses. If you are setting up a MIG machine instead, read MIG settings without a door chart and the MIG calculator.

Limits

These figures are starting points from manufacturer guidance, not a welding procedure. Code or structural work needs a qualified procedure and your machine manual applies first.

Frequently asked questions

What tungsten size for 125 amps?
Per Miller’s table, 3/32 in 2% tungsten covers 125–200 A, and 1/16 in covers up to 140 A.
What is the amp range of 1/8 inch tungsten?
About 150–325 A for 2% alloyed tungsten and 190–240 A for pure tungsten.
How many amps to TIG weld 1/8 inch steel?
A common starting point is about 125 A, adjusted with the foot pedal.
How do I grind tungsten for DC?
Grind lengthwise to a point so the arc stays stable and focused.
How much argon flow does TIG need?
Typically about 15–25 cfh. More can pull air into the arc.
