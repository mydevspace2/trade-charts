Voltage Drop and the 3% Rule: When to Upsize Wire

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

The famous “3% rule” comes from informational notes in the NEC, not from an enforceable requirement. It is still good engineering, and some equipment manuals make it mandatory.

What the Code says

Informational notes in Articles 210 (branch circuits) and 215 (feeders) recommend sizing conductors so voltage drop does not exceed 3% on a branch circuit or feeder, and 5% for feeder plus branch circuit combined. Informational notes explain; they are not requirements unless your jurisdiction adopts them as such. Specific rules (for example fire pump circuits) do set mandatory limits.

The calculation

VD = K × L × R × I ÷ 1000, K = 2 (single-phase) or 1.732 (three-phase)

R is conductor resistance in ohms per 1,000 ft from NEC Chapter 9 Table 8, L the one-way length in feet and I the current in amps. Example: a 30 A, 240 V condenser circuit 120 ft from the panel on 10 AWG copper (1.24 Ω/kft): 2 × 120 × 1.24 × 30 ÷ 1000 = 8.9 V, or 3.7%. Upsizing to 8 AWG (0.778 Ω/kft) gives 5.6 V, 2.3%.

When to check

  • Detached garages, shops and outbuildings
  • Well pumps and irrigation
  • EV chargers on long runs
  • Rooftop units and condensers far from the panel
  • Low-voltage DC (solar, RV, battery) where a few volts is a big percentage

Watch the details

  • Use the actual load current for voltage drop, not the breaker size, unless you want a worst case.
  • Table 8 is DC resistance; for large AC conductors in steel raceway, Table 9 impedance is more accurate.
  • Upsizing ungrounded conductors for voltage drop requires a proportional increase of the equipment grounding conductor (250.122(B)).

DC and low-voltage circuits

On 12 V and 24 V DC systems, a few volts of drop is a large percentage, so wire size is often driven by voltage drop rather than ampacity. Use the single-phase/DC setting in the calculator, which uses the round-trip length (2 × one-way).
