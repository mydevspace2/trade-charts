How to Read a PT Chart (Dew, Bubble and Glide Explained)

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

A PT chart turns a gauge reading into a temperature. That one conversion is behind every superheat, subcooling and coil-temperature check you make, so it is worth getting exactly right.

1. What the chart tells you

When liquid and vapor refrigerant are both present (in the evaporator and condenser), the refrigerant is saturated: its pressure and temperature are locked together. The PT chart lists that pair for every temperature. R-410A at 40 °F, for example, saturates at 118.8 psig. If your suction gauge reads 119 psig, the refrigerant boiling in the evaporator is at about 40 °F.

The chart does not tell you the temperature of vapor that has left the coil or of liquid that has cooled below saturation. That difference is exactly what superheat and subcooling measure.

2. Gauge pressure, absolute pressure and vacuum

Field charts, including ours, list psig: pressure above the surrounding atmosphere. Absolute pressure (psia) is psig plus about 14.7 at sea level. Below atmospheric pressure the chart switches to inches of mercury vacuum (inHg). At altitude the atmosphere is lower, so a gauge reads a little higher than a sea-level chart for the same temperature, roughly 0.5 psi per 1,000 ft; for low-pressure refrigerants that matters more than for R-410A.

3. Single refrigerants vs blends

Pure refrigerants (R-22, R-32, R-134a) and azeotropes (R-507A, R-513A) have one pressure per temperature. Zeotropic blends (R-454B, R-407C, R-448A and most 400-series blends) boil over a range of temperatures at a given pressure. The chart then shows two columns:

  • Bubble point: the temperature where liquid starts to boil (first bubble).
  • Dew point: the temperature where the last liquid has evaporated (last drop).

The difference is the glide. R-454B at 40 °F shows 107.5 psig dew and 113.2 psig bubble, about a 2.7 °F glide. R-407C has a much larger glide, about 10.8 °F at the same pressure level.

4. Which column to use

Superheat uses the dew column. Refrigerant leaving the evaporator has finished boiling at the dew point, so superheat = suction line temperature − dew temperature. Subcooling uses the bubble column. Refrigerant leaving the condenser is fully liquid below the bubble point, so subcooling = bubble temperature − liquid line temperature. Mixing them up on a high-glide blend can make a correct charge look several degrees off.

5. Worked example (R-410A)

Suction: 118 psig = 40 °F saturated. Suction line 52 °F. Superheat = 12 °F.
Liquid: 340 psig ≈ 105 °F saturated. Liquid line 95 °F. Subcooling = 10 °F.
Compare superheat with the target (fixed orifice) and subcooling with the rating plate (TXV/EEV). Our superheat and subcooling calculators do the lookups for 32 refrigerants.

6. Common mistakes

  • Reading the wrong refrigerant scale (check the rating plate, not the sticker on the tank).
  • Measuring pressure and temperature at different places.
  • Judging charge before the system has run 15 minutes, or with dirty filters and coils.
  • Using a standing (off) pressure to judge charge: it only tells you the refrigerant is present and roughly the ambient temperature.
  • Ignoring non-condensables: pressure higher than the chart predicts for a system at rest can mean air in the system.

Frequently asked questions

Is a PT chart in psig or psia?
Field PT charts, including ours, are in psig (gauge pressure). Absolute pressure is psig plus about 14.7 psi at sea level. Below 0 psig the chart shows inches of mercury vacuum.
Which column do I use for superheat on a blend?
The dew column. Superheat is measured from the point where the last liquid has boiled off, which is the dew point.
Which column do I use for subcooling on a blend?
The bubble column. Subcooling is measured from the point where the vapor has fully condensed, which is the bubble point.
Can I judge the charge from standing pressure?
No. With the system off, pressure only tells you refrigerant is present and roughly the ambient temperature. Judge charge with the system running, using superheat or subcooling.
Why does my gauge read higher than the chart for a system at rest?
If the system has equalized at a known ambient temperature and the pressure is clearly above the chart value, non-condensables such as air, or the wrong refrigerant, are possible causes.
