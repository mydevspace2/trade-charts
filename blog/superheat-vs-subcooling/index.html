Superheat vs Subcooling: Which to Charge By and Why

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

Both numbers tell you where the refrigerant is changing state. Superheat looks at the evaporator outlet, subcooling at the condenser outlet. Which one you charge by depends on the metering device.

Superheat

Superheat is how far the suction vapor is above its saturated (dew) temperature. Low superheat means liquid may be reaching the compressor (floodback); high superheat means the evaporator is starving, often from low charge, a restriction or low load. On fixed-orifice systems superheat changes with indoor and outdoor conditions, which is why manufacturers give a target superheat table based on indoor wet bulb and outdoor dry bulb. Our target superheat calculator uses the widely published approximation of those tables.

Subcooling

Subcooling is how far the liquid is below its saturated (bubble) temperature. It shows how much liquid is stacked in the condenser. A TXV or EEV holds evaporator superheat roughly constant on its own, so on those systems the charge shows up in subcooling instead, and manufacturers print a target subcooling on the rating plate or in the installation instructions.

Before you touch the charge

  1. Confirm airflow: clean filter, correct blower speed, coils clean.
  2. Run the system at least 15 minutes, ideally in a stable test mode.
  3. Measure pressure and temperature at the same location, with insulated clamps on clean copper.
  4. Use the right PT column: dew for superheat, bubble for subcooling on blends.
  5. Adjust in small steps and let the system settle between changes. On new installs weigh in the charge, including the line-set adjustment.

What the combinations suggest

SuperheatSubcoolingCommon causes to check
HighLowUndercharge, leak
LowHighOvercharge
HighHighRestriction (filter drier, TXV), non-condensables possible
LowLowLow indoor airflow or load, TXV overfeeding, compressor issue

These are starting points for diagnosis, not verdicts: confirm with temperature splits, amperage and the manufacturer’s chart. Use our superheat and subcooling calculators in the field.

Worked example: piston system

An R-410A split with a fixed orifice. Indoor return wet bulb 63 °F, outdoor air entering the condenser 85 °F. The target superheat calculator gives (3 × 63 − 80 − 85) ÷ 2 = 12 °F. Suction pressure converts to 40 °F saturated, and the suction line at the outdoor unit reads 58 °F: 18 °F superheat, 6 °F above target. With airflow and coils confirmed, that points to a low charge; add refrigerant slowly and recheck after the system settles.

Why airflow comes first

Both readings depend on heat transfer. Low indoor airflow reduces the load on the evaporator, so superheat falls and it can look like an overcharge; a dirty outdoor coil raises head pressure and changes subcooling. Correct airflow (typically about 350–400 CFM per ton unless the manufacturer says otherwise), clean coils and a clean filter before adjusting the charge. Then use the PT chart for the exact refrigerant on the rating plate.

A note on A2L systems

R-454B and R-32 systems are charged the same way, but service practices change: A2L-rated recovery and leak-detection equipment, no ignition sources, and the manufacturer’s instructions for refrigerant detection sensors and mitigation controls.

Frequently asked questions

Do I charge a TXV system by superheat or subcooling?
By subcooling. The TXV holds superheat roughly constant, so extra refrigerant shows up as subcooling. Use the target on the rating plate or in the installation manual.
Do I charge a piston (fixed orifice) system by superheat or subcooling?
By superheat, compared with the manufacturer’s target superheat table based on indoor wet bulb and outdoor dry bulb.
What does high superheat and low subcooling usually mean?
Most often an undercharge or a leak. Confirm with airflow checks and the manufacturer’s chart before adding refrigerant.
How long should the system run before I take readings?
At least 15 minutes, ideally in a stable test mode, so pressures and temperatures settle.
Does airflow affect superheat?
Yes. Low indoor airflow lowers the load on the evaporator and lowers superheat, which can look like an overcharge. Fix airflow before touching the charge.
