Charging a Heat Pump in Heating Mode: Rules of Thumb and Limits

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

Checking the charge on a heat pump in winter is where a lot of good techs get stuck. Subcooling and superheat targets are written for cooling mode, the outdoor coil has turned into the evaporator, and many manufacturers say plainly not to charge by gauges at all below a certain outdoor temperature. This guide covers what the manuals say, the field rules of thumb that help you decide whether to dig deeper, and the checks that come before touching the charge.

Start with the manufacturer

Every source we read agrees on one thing: follow the installation manual. The details differ by brand and by model, so there is no universal heating-mode charging table.

  • Trane installer manuals call subcooling in cooling mode the only recommended method above 55 °F outdoor temperature. Below 55 °F the only recommended method is to weigh in the charge in heating mode, and they note it is important to return in spring or summer to charge accurately in cooling mode.
  • Daikin heat pump instructions require the outdoor temperature to be 60 °F or higher for gauge checks, and state that the proper method of charging in heat mode is by weight with adjustments for line size and length.
  • Lennox uses 60 °F as the dividing line: above it, adjust the charge in cooling mode; below it, use the heating mode procedure in its charging bulletin.
  • A 17 SEER R-454B heat pump charging label (A7HP14F18P) lists subcooling targets in heating mode below 65 °F ambient, with 20 minutes of run time and indoor return air in a stated range.

The dividing line is anywhere from 55 to 65 °F depending on the maker. Write down which one your unit uses before you hook up gauges.

Do the obvious checks first

Bryan Orr of HVAC School, writing in ACHR News, lists two steps before any measurement: get any frost off the outdoor coil completely, then check the filter, coils and blower wheel. In heating mode the roles swap. The indoor coil is the condenser, so a dirty filter can cause extremely high head pressure, and a dirty outdoor coil can cause low suction pressure. Both symptoms look like charge problems and are not.

Heat-mode rules of thumb

These are diagnostic hints from the same article, not charging targets. They apply with a frost-free coil and outdoor temperatures between 15 and 65 °F.

CheckExpected (rule of thumb)
Discharge line temperature100–110 °F above outdoor temperature
Suction line temperature5–15 °F cooler than outdoor temperature
Suction saturation temperature20–25 °F below outdoor ambient (R-410A)
Liquid line temperature3–15 °F warmer than indoor temperature
Condensing temperature30–40 °F above indoor dry bulb
Superheat (system with heat-mode TXV)about 8–14 °F; subcooling often higher than in cooling

Orr stresses that these only tell you whether to connect gauges and look further. If anything looks off, verify with gauges and the manufacturer data.

Worked example on R-410A

Say it is 50 °F outside. The 20–25 °F rule puts suction saturation at 25–30 °F. On the R-410A PT chart that is about 87.4–97.0 psig at the suction port. If your gauge reads far below that with a clean coil and good indoor airflow, the system may be low on charge or have a restriction. If the indoor side is at 70 °F, the 30–40 °F rule puts condensing at 100–110 °F, which is about 317.6 psig at 100 °F and a little higher at 110 °F. Use the subcooling calculator once you have a liquid pressure and line temperature.

Notice that none of this tells you how many ounces to add. It tells you whether the numbers are plausible. That is the honest limit of heating-mode gauge checks.

Weighing in

If you have repaired a leak or replaced a component, recover the charge and weigh in the nameplate amount plus line-set adjustments. Orr points out that weighing out and in is sensible after a circuit repair, but silly when you only need to confirm the system is working. For a simple check, the rules of thumb above plus a return visit in mild or warm weather is more practical.

Common mistakes

  • Using cooling-mode subcooling targets in heating mode.
  • Reading a frosted outdoor coil, which depresses suction pressure.
  • Reading the wrong refrigerant scale, especially now that R-454B and R-32 systems are arriving; see R-454B service basics.
  • Adding charge during defrost or right after it. Allow about 15 minutes of normal operation afterwards (Carrier heating check chart).
  • Ignoring airflow. Fix the filter and blower before touching refrigerant; see superheat vs subcooling.

Frequently asked questions

Can I charge a heat pump in heating mode?
Only by following the manufacturer. Trane says to weigh in the charge below 55 °F outdoor, Daikin says the proper heat-mode method is by weight, and Lennox has a specific heating-mode procedure below 60 °F.
At what outdoor temperature should I switch to cooling mode to charge?
It depends on the maker: Trane 55 °F, Lennox and Daikin 60 °F, and an R-454B heat pump label 65 °F. Check the unit’s manual.
What discharge line temperature is normal in heating mode?
ACHR News gives a rule of thumb of 100–110 °F above the outdoor temperature, as a diagnostic hint rather than a charging target.
Why does a dirty filter cause high head pressure in heating mode?
Because the indoor coil is the condenser in heating mode, so low airflow across it raises condensing pressure.
Should I weigh in the charge after a repair?
Yes. After a refrigerant circuit repair, recovering and weighing in the nameplate charge plus line-set adjustment is the sound approach.
