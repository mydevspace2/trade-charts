Non-Condensables: Reading High Standing Pressure and Head Pressure

Guide · Updated November 13, 2026

High head pressure has a long list of causes, and air in the system is near the bottom of it. When the common causes are ruled out, though, non-condensables are the next suspect, and the PT chart gives you a clean way to prove it. This guide explains how air gets in, how it shows up on your gauges, and how to confirm it.

What non-condensables are and how they get in

ACHR News describes air as nitrogen, oxygen and moisture, all harmful to a refrigeration system. Air does not condense, so it collects at the top of the condenser and takes up surface the refrigerant needs. It can enter in two ways:

  • Poor service practice: not purging hoses, or not fully evacuating after the system was opened to replace a part or fix a leak.
  • A low-side leak: if enough refrigerant escapes that the low side runs below atmospheric pressure, air is pulled in. Topping off without recovering and evacuating leaves that air trapped.

The oxygen and moisture can react with the oil and, when heated, form acids that attack motor windings and cause corrosion and sludge, so this is more than a performance issue.

Rule out the common causes first

Marchese (Heatcraft), writing in ACHR News, says non-condensables are not a very common cause and that techs should first look at dirty coils, insufficient condenser airflow and recirculation of condenser air. Overcharge is the other look-alike. Our guide to superheat and subcooling shows how an overcharge reads, and the PT chart guide covers the basics.

Symptoms

ACHR News’ Professor lists the symptoms of air in a system as high discharge temperature, high condensing pressure, high condensing subcooling, high condenser split, high compression ratio, normal to slightly high suction pressure, normal superheat and high amp draw. With a TXV, those look very much like an overcharge, so you need the saturation test below to tell them apart.

The standing-pressure test (system off)

  1. Identify the refrigerant.
  2. Turn the compressor off but keep the condenser fan running, so everything comes to the same temperature.
  3. Put probes on the discharge line, the liquid line and the air entering the condenser.
  4. When all three read the same temperature, record the condenser pressure.
  5. Convert that pressure to a saturation temperature on the PT chart for that refrigerant.
  6. If the converted temperature is higher than the measured temperatures by more than a few degrees, there are non-condensables.

That is the procedure from the Marchese article. Parker’s P-T analysis guide explains the physics: the foreign gas adds its own pressure to the refrigerant pressure, so the actual refrigerant temperature in the condenser is lower than the chart suggests for that pressure.

Worked example on R-410A

After a long shutdown, all three probes read 80 °F. On the R-410A PT chart 80 °F saturates at about 235.7 psig. If your gauge reads 275.7 psig, the pressure corresponds to roughly 90 °F, about 10 °F higher than the actual temperature, and air is a strong suspect. A hose that was not purged is a classic reason. One article suggests that a difference of more than 10 psig on an equalized system often indicates air; treat that as a hint, not a rule.

Fixing it

For a small system with a recovery machine, recover the refrigerant, replace the filter drier if the system was open, pull a deep vacuum and weigh in the nameplate charge. Venting refrigerant to purge air is not acceptable under EPA rules. Danfoss’ troubleshooting guidance for larger systems describes purging the condenser with a reclaim system, running the system to operating temperature and purging again if necessary. For A2L systems use equipment rated for them; see A2L tools.

Prevention

  • Purge hoses, and evacuate to a deep vacuum with a micron gauge before opening the circuit to refrigerant.
  • Fix low-side leaks before topping off.
  • Record the standing-pressure reading at commissioning so you have a baseline.

Frequently asked questions

What are non-condensables?
Gases such as air (nitrogen, oxygen and moisture) that do not condense in the refrigerant circuit and collect at the top of the condenser.
How do air and non-condensables get into a system?
Through poor service practice, such as not purging hoses or not evacuating after opening the system, or through a low-side leak that lets the low side go below atmospheric pressure.
How do I test for non-condensables?
With the compressor off and the condenser fan running, wait until the discharge line, liquid line and entering air are the same temperature, convert the pressure to saturation temperature, and see whether it is more than a few degrees higher.
Is it just an overcharge?
With a TXV the symptoms are similar, including high head pressure and high subcooling. The standing-pressure test separates the two.
Can I vent the air out?
Do not vent refrigerant. Recover it, evacuate and recharge by weight, or use a reclaim-based purge on larger systems.
