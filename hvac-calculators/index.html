Which calculator do I need?

TXV or EEV system: charge by subcooling at the value on the rating plate, then confirm superheat is reasonable. Fixed orifice / piston: charge by superheat against a target that depends on indoor wet bulb and outdoor dry bulb. In every case, make sure airflow and coil condition are right before touching the charge, and weigh in the charge when installing.

Data behind the calculators

All three calculators read the same saturation tables as our PT charts: 32 refrigerants computed with a REFPROP-equivalent equation of state (or taken from the manufacturer chart) and compared point by point with Arkema, Honeywell, Hudson Technologies and NIST data. For blends, superheat uses the dew point and subcooling uses the bubble point automatically, so you do not have to remember which column applies.

Typical workflow on a service call

  1. Confirm the refrigerant and metering device from the rating plate and the indoor coil.
  2. Check airflow, filters and coils before anything else.
  3. Run the system 15 minutes; record suction and liquid pressures and line temperatures.
  4. Calculate superheat and subcooling and compare with the target or rating plate.
  5. Adjust charge slowly, wait for the system to settle, and re-measure.

Field notes and examples: superheat vs subcooling, how to read a PT chart.

Which HVAC calculator should you use?

Superheat and subcooling are the two numbers that tell you whether a system is charged correctly. Which one you check depends on the metering device. On a fixed orifice or piston system, charge by superheat. On a TXV or EEV system, charge by subcooling. Both need the refrigerant’s saturation temperature at the measured pressure, which is what the PT charts give you.

A basic charging workflow

  1. Let the system run 10 to 15 minutes so pressures settle.
  2. Clamp a thermometer on the suction line near the service valve, and another on the liquid line.
  3. Read suction and liquid pressures and look up the saturation temperatures for the refrigerant on the nameplate.
  4. Superheat is suction line temperature minus suction saturation temperature. Subcooling is liquid saturation temperature minus liquid line temperature.
  5. Compare with the manufacturer’s target. Add or recover refrigerant in small steps, waiting for readings to stabilize between each.

Before you blame the charge

Dirty filters, a dirty evaporator or condenser coil, low airflow and a bad blower capacitor can all mimic charge problems. Fix airflow first, then check the charge. For background, read superheat vs subcooling and how to read a PT chart. Always follow the equipment manufacturer’s charging instructions and EPA rules for handling refrigerant.

Worked example: reading both numbers on an R-410A system

Suction pressure 118 psig and suction line temperature 52 °F: the dew temperature at 118 psig is about 40 °F, so superheat is about 12 °F. Liquid pressure 335 psig and liquid line temperature 95 °F: the bubble temperature at 335 psig is about 104 °F, so subcooling is about 9 °F. Compare both with the manufacturer’s targets before touching the charge.

When the readings disagree

If superheat and subcooling both look wrong in the same direction, suspect airflow or a dirty coil before the charge. If they point in opposite directions, a restriction or a metering device problem is more likely than a simple low or high charge. Change one thing at a time and recheck after the system settles.
